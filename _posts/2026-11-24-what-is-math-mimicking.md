title: What is math mimicking?
description: Your students can follow the steps. Can they tell you why they work? Here's the habit behind that gap, and one move to start closing it tomorrow.
---

Picture a student solving ¾ ÷ ½. They write "keep, change, flip," multiply, and land on 1½. Right answer. Now ask them *why* flipping works, or what 1½ actually means here.

For a lot of students, that's where the room goes quiet.

That gap between getting the answer and understanding it is what we call **math mimicking**.

## Two sides of one habit

Math mimicking has two sides, and they feed each other.

**The teaching habit.** Most of us learned math by watching someone show the steps, then copying them. So that's how many of us teach: *I do, we do, you do.* The teacher models a procedure, and students practice imitating it.

**The student result.** Students get very good at following along. They can reproduce a procedure on a worksheet, but they can't explain it, adapt it, or tell when it doesn't apply. Change one number on the test and they're lost.

None of this is a teacher problem. It's a habit almost all of us inherited. And habits can change.

## What sense-making looks like instead

The opposite of mimicking is **sense-making**: students reasoning about what's going on, not just which steps come next.

Here's the same problem, made sense of:

> How many halves fit in ¾? One whole half fits, with ¼ left over. That ¼ is half of another half. So the answer is 1½.

Same answer. Completely different understanding. And a student who thinks this way can handle ⅚ ÷ ⅓ tomorrow without needing a new trick.

| Mimicking | Sense-making |
|---|---|
| Copy the teacher's steps | Ask why the steps work |
| One right method | Many strategies, compared and connected |
| Getting the answer | Explaining the reasoning |
| The teacher does the thinking | Students do the thinking |

## One move to try tomorrow

After a student gives a correct answer, don't move on. Ask:

> "Why does that work?"

Then wait. Give the class a moment to think, and invite a second student to add on. You'll learn quickly who's mimicking and who's making sense, and your students will learn that in your room, *why* matters as much as *what*.
